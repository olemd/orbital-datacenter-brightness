What would orbital rings look like from your sky?

Continuous rings around the Earth, at the altitudes and tilts you choose, lit by the Sun, shadowed by the Earth, and drawn against the real stars and a modelled twilight sky for your latitude, date and time. Everything is computed in your browser. Drag the sky to look around, or focus it and use the arrow keys.

What this is

Rings, not satellites. Each ring is a continuous band all the way round the Earth, in the spirit of Paul Birch's orbital rings: a circle at the altitude you set, with a width and an albedo. The marked guide lines show where each ring is, solid where it is sunlit and dotted where it is in Earth's shadow. The white line in the sky is what you would actually see.

Where you can see them. An equatorial ring hugs the celestial equator and is low in the sky for most people. It sets below the horizon entirely beyond a latitude that depends on its height: about 17° for a ring at 300 km, 40° at 2,000 km, 63° at 7,500 km. A tilted ring reaches higher latitudes and, because its plane is fixed against the stars, it swings across the sky through the night as the stars do. An equatorial ring looks the same all night, and only the Earth's shadow moves along it.

Node RA is the right ascension of the ascending node: where a tilted ring crosses the celestial equator heading north, in degrees along the equator, as of the date you give.

Precession. The Earth's equatorial bulge turns a tilted ring's plane about the pole, the same effect that keeps sun-synchronous satellites in step with the Sun. For a ring moving at orbital speed the node drifts by 1.5 n J2 (RE/a)2 cos i per unit time: about 2.7° a day westward for a ring at 2,000 km tilted 45°, so within weeks it moves from the evening sky to the morning sky. A Birch ring's rotor moves faster than orbital speed and would precess at a different rate, and a ring could be steered to hold its node, so you can turn this off. Equatorial and polar rings do not drift in any way you could see.

Fixed to the stars, or to the ground. Paul Birch's orbital rings are not left to drift: each is made to precess once a day so that it "will hover above any meridian selected on the surface of Earth", with stations that "stay in one place above some designated point on Earth" (Wikipedia). A ring fixed to the ground is that design. It holds still in your sky all night, and only the Earth's shadow moves along it. You place it by where it crosses the equator, in degrees of longitude east of you. A ring fixed to the stars is a passive ring instead, and it swings across the sky with the stars. An equatorial ring looks the same either way.

Stations and elevator cables. A ground-fixed ring can carry stations, spaced evenly around it from the node, with an elevator cable hanging straight down from each to the ground. Each station is drawn as a point of light the size of a diffuse surface of the given width. Each cable is treated like the ring, as a line of the given thickness. Its lower part falls into the Earth's shadow first after sunset, so the lit part shrinks upward through the evening. No sizes for these are published, so treat them as yours to choose.

Brightness. Each piece of ring is treated as a diffuse reflector of its own area, using the same Lambertian formula the satellite page uses (Boley, Lawler & Rein 2026, arXiv:2608.02757, equation 2). Real structures would have their own shape, materials and specular glints, which are not modelled. The width and albedo are yours to set; nothing here is a design.

Visibility. The eye resolves about one arcminute, so a ring counts as visible where a patch that size is brighter than the faintest point of light you could see against the sky at that spot. The eye picks out lines a little more easily than points, so faint rings may be somewhat easier to see than this says. Rings wide enough to resolve are drawn as bands of their angular width, seen face-on, with their light spread across them. Very wide bands are capped at about 190 pixels across.

Sky and stars come from the satellite page: twilight sky brightness calibrated at the zenith and modelled across the dome, artificial skyglow from the light pollution setting, and 8,912 real stars from the HYG catalog. The Moon is not modelled.

Sun time, not clock time. Times are local solar time: noon is when the Sun is highest.
